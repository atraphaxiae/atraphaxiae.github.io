Starting this *completionist's journal* with an album review. This first entry will be me reviewing
**The Real McCoy** by McCoy Tyner, which is in my opinion, one of the best post-bop albums there
are. This was THE album that finally fully got me into jazz. Before this, I was a massive fusion
person. Casiopea, Head Hunters, Spectrum, The Inner Mounting Flame, you know the type. Hovering
above the jazz genre, dipping my toes into Kind of Blue, A Love Supreme, Moanin', but not really
*getting* it. I was constantly listening to those three albums, which RateYourMusic rates as the
best jazz albums, but they never really reeled me in.

But this album changed all of that. This album was my gateway drug into jazz. If I had to choose
five albums to take to a desert island, it would be this, Lee Morgan's Search for the New Land,
Wayne Shorter's Speak No Evil, Art Blakey's Free for All, and John Coltrane's A Love Supreme. All
post-bop excursions by the way, did I mention that I love post-bop?

Released in 1967, this album features McCoy Tyner, Joe Henderson, Ron Carter, and Elvin Jones. Two
of whom were part of the Coltrane quartet, so you know this is gonna be a post-bop masterclass.
Containing five tracks, two of which I would consider in the list of best post-bop pieces:
**Passion Dance** and **Contemplation**.

Passion Dance might just be the best opener of an album. Like, it's the most first track ever.
Jones' intro solo instantly sets the mood for what you're going to get. Then everyone else comes in,
and it's like some kind of parade. Henderson and Tyner are playing the overall theme of the song,
and Carter's dancing gracefully between all the notes. You also instantly get Tyner's signature
chord slamming literally not even ten seconds in. I particularly love Henderson's solo in this. Some
people don't like how he honks, shrieks, growls and squeals in his solos, but they're wrong. He's
tearing the hell out of this piece, and they don't like it? Then we get the incredible outro of the
song, with all four men seemingly battling each other for the spotlight, as they fade out, taking
the parade to another place. This song in general consistently just makes me super energized and
happy.

Then all the happiness is thrown out once we get to the next track, Contemplation. This is actually
where I got the name idea for this journal section, which is a portmanteau of *contemplation* and
*completion*. I don't know why Tyner called this song *Contemplation*, because I'm not able to
contemplate when I'm literally crying. This song is soul-crushing, melancholic, and sad in just
about all the right ways. This song consists of: Henderson crying his saxophone out, Tyner crying
his piano out, and Carter picking up the pieces in the end. All the while, Jones is the constant
motivation to keep on going forward with his unrelenting triplets. Another great song, and probably
the one I keep repeating the most out of all the songs in this album.

Moving on to Side B with **Four by Five** coming up first. Nothing really notable to say about this
song, other than that it's a nice post-bop piece with great solos from Henderson, Tyner, and Jones.
There are some weird time signature shenanigans in this one, which is probably why Tyner called it
*Four by Five*.

Next up is **Search for Peace**. This is another great melancholic song, which I think is a more
contemplative song compared to *Contemplation*. The solos of Tyner and Henderson are both incredible
here. Tyner's arpeggios sound like clear droplets of rain, contrasted by his left hand, still
slamming here but more quiet now. Henderson really shines in these kinds of balladic songs; the way
he solos here is just so soft, sensual, sweet and gentle, and his tone is full too. Like some kind
of creamy flowing chocolate melting in your mouth. And then Jones is the one putting the ambience
on, his brushwork either sounding like the rain, or being your footsteps while walking forward.

Finally, we have **Blues on the Corner**. This one is the most bop and bluesy sounding one of the
bunch, with a catchy theme akin to Moanin' and the like, but that doesn't mean that this piece isn't
good. This has a very unique bridge-like part at the start of Henderson's solo which I really love,
where Henderson is repeating the same phrase with minimal variation while Carter is just repeating
the same two notes for a couple of bars. In my head, I can visualize Henderson playfully skipping
around the place while Carter is just walking, following behind him. Then Tyner and Jones catch up
to them, Henderson still skipping forward. After his solo, they all finally start walking in sync,
and they end the album with a satisfying explosion.

I wrote this review while listening to PBTHAL's vinyl rip of the BN Classic release of the album.
Sadly, there is a bit of IGD here especially in Henderson's solo in Blues on the Corner. Ugh, why is
it always the saxophone that gets affected by this. If you have a vinyl rip of the Music Matters
release of this album, please let me know! I will be very grateful, especially since this is one of
my favorite albums ever.

Overall, I rate this album supreme a mighty juicy **5/5**. I highly recommend that you go check this
album out!
