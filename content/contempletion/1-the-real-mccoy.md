## On Post-Bop
I used to not really 'get' jazz. I remember listening to Herbie Hancock's *Empyrean Isles* once, and
I was like: "Yeah that sounds good, but it's not really engaging to me." Back then I was big on jazz
fusion; the reason I was checking out Herbie Hancock was because I was super enamored by
*Head Hunters* that time. I also listened to Casiopea, PRISM, and the many other dime-a-dozen
Japanese fusion bands there are. Though personally, I wasn't a fan of the 80s stuff that had a lot
more electronic stuff in it. I like my music better when it's acoustic.

I decided to branch out in my listening, so I hit RateYourMusic to find the quote-unquote best jazz
albums. I tried *Kind of Blue*, *A Love Supreme*, and Art Blakey's *Moanin'*, but they never really
left an impression on me. Except for the self-titled first track of *Moanin'*; I immediately thought
that was a masterpiece. When I was listening to *A Love Supreme* though, I focused more on the piano
than on Coltrane. His style of slamming the chords really reeled me in, so I looked him up on RYM
and sure enough, he also has a bolded album on there.

*The Real McCoy* was my gateway drug to jazz. Listening to this album completely turned my music
world upside down; in an instant it became my favorite album. Pretty much because of its first two
tracks, which are perfect in every way. This finally opened my ears, and then I found myself
listening to *Search for the New Land*, *Speak No Evil*, *Free for All*, and many other Post-Bop
albums. If it's Post-Bop, Blue Note, and from the mid-60s, I would probably love it.

I'm listening to this album primarily using a vinyl rip of the BN Classic release. This is the best
version I can reasonably get, and it sounds way better than any other release. But, if you know of a
vinyl rip of the Music Matters release of it, please point me in that direction by sending me an
email :>

## 1.01 Passion Dance
*Passion Dance* is the first track, and the first big highlight of this album. It has an intro
drum solo from Elvin Jones, which is unmistakeable for this song and instantly gets me focused on
listening. Even after a few years of almost constantly listening to this song, when my brain hears
that opening drum solo, it says "it's THAT song!" Then we get to the main theme of the song,
which is a very memorable and fun two-part call-and-response, both between Joe Henderson (saxophone)
and Ron Carter (double bass), and between McCoy Tyner (piano, right hand) and McCoy Tyner (piano,
left hand).

After that, it's Tyner's solo. A lot of nice pentatonic scales and arpeggios, typical for him, but
what I'm really here for is his chord slamming. In nearly every single one of his solos I've heard,
there is this part near the end where he slams chords. It's pure ecstasy, and it gives a lot of
build-up for the next section of the song.

In this case, next up is Henderson's solo. He doesn't let any of Tyner's build-up go to waste,
starting his solo off with two super strong high notes. I don't know why, but his style of buzzing
and honking saxophone really works well in almost every album he's in. This song got me trying to
collect all the Blue Note albums that he's in, just to listen to his saxophone style. He ends his
solo in a way which I can only describe as 'Celtic'. Not sure why, but that's what I think when I
hear the end of his solo.

Then we get Jones' drum solo, a showcase of his never-ending skill. Honestly though, I'm a bigger
fan of how he constantly pushes the song forward with his unrelenting triplets. I don't think a
different drummer could comp with this song as perfectly as he does. We get a callback to the main
theme, then finally, all members close the song in a sort of musical battle, gradually moving away
from the listener and fading out, taking the music to a different place.

## 1.02 Contemplation
*Contemplation* is the next track, and the second big highlight of this album. As time went on, I
found myself listening to this piece more than *Passion Dance*, mostly because I feel more things
from this piece now that I'm in a harder stage of my life. I don't know why Tyner named this piece
this way, because this really isn't something I want to hear when I'm contemplating things. Not
because I don't like this piece, but because it's just so sad and melancholic, making me look at my
life and contemplate all my decisions like everything is sad and depressing.

I love the intro of this song. You have Carter and Jones, and then Tyner further in. I love the
minimalistic two notes from the bass that go on for the entirety of the intro, that first, crispy
hi-hat splash from Jones, and the feathery arpeggios of Tyner on the piano. Then the main theme of
the song comes in from the saxophone.

I really love Henderson's tone here. He's belching out from his saxophone, but each note is as clear
as day. When I hear him on this track, I can feel him screaming out into the void, like he's
desperate for someone or something to just hear his cries for help. After the theme, Tyner speaks
for a bit, like a friend letting him know that he's there to help. The rest of the song feels like
a sorrowful conversation between friends for me. Henderson's solo is pretty short, a response to
what Tyner said moments before. He's a bit more calm here, like he's conversing instead of screaming
now that he has someone to talk to. He gives space for Tyner to talk at the end of his solo.

McCoy's solo here takes up almost half of the song, but honestly, it's not enough. It's just too
beautiful; the chords that he uses as a callback to the main theme are so satisfyingly tearjerking,
like they're the words that you've been wanting to hear all this time. Around the end of his solo he
starts slamming the same chord over and over again, like he's trying to get something through to
his friend's head. Carter also speaks up in the space Tyner left him at the end of his solo. He
isn't really known for his soloing, but I think his solo here is one of the best bass solos of all
time, and it fits the conversational style perfectly.

Jones doesn't get a solo, but he's been here from the start. He's in the background, supporting his
friend, and being the driving force that's always pushing him forward, telling him to never give up.
Henderson ultimately accepts his situation, and after screaming out to the void again with the main
theme, he finally starts to fade out, walking away with his three friends behind him.

## The Others
Side B of the album starts off with **Four by Five**, which is speedy and has some time signature
shenanigans in its main theme. You have great solos from Henderson, Tyner, and Jones, and I
especially like the short trading section between Tyner and Jones here. Overall a great Post-Bop
piece, but it's the track that I personally don't find myself listening to very much.

**Search for Peace** is another melancholic piece, this time in a ballad style. I love the wispy and
light sound that everyone makes on this track. Tyner's solo sounds like intermittent raindrops on
a still lake. Henderson generally eats ballads up with his soloing, the sound he makes with his
saxophone so fragile, but so full and pristine at the same time.

**Blues on the Corner** is like a twin to *Passion Dance*; I find it completes and ends the album
nicely and on a high note. I especially love the start to Henderson's solo where he and Carter
repeat the same two note phrase over and over again but in different pitches. It's a nice departure
from the bluesy style that this piece is centered around. Finally, Jones ends the album just as he
started it, with a short drum solo.

## Final Thoughts
To this day, *The Real McCoy* is still one of my favorite albums. I would even go as far as saying
that if I crashed on a desert island, I would be very happy even if this album was the only one that
survived. This is a very significant entry in my jazz listening journey, and it has opened many
doors for me, finally getting me to love jazz. Also, I forgot to mention, the track *Contemplation*
is where I got the inspiration for the title of this blog, which is a portmanteau of 'completion'
and 'contemplation'. I just love that piece so much, if it isn't already obvious.

Anyways, I absolutely recommend you listen to this album. I'm confident that you won't be
disappointed, so just do it, especially if you haven't given jazz a try yet. Overall, I'd give this
album a high and juicy **5/5**. Truly the real McCoy of Post-Bop albums.
